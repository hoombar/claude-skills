# Ben Website Writing Style Profile

This is the bundled writing-style reference for generating posts for `benpearson.dev`. If this conflicts with a direct instruction from Ben in the current conversation, follow the current instruction.

## Core Principle

The writing should feel like clear written conversation: reflective, plain-spoken, personally owned, and lightly polished, but not corporate, generic, or over-produced.

The goal is not to imitate every quirk from casual email. The goal is to produce publishable article prose that preserves Ben's judgement, uncertainty, rhythm, and practical way of thinking.

## Anchor Paragraph

> I think AI is useful when it helps me tidy up the structure without taking over the writing. It can point out the weak spots, make the argument easier to follow, and help me work out what I was trying to say in the first place. That still leaves the judgement with me, which is probably the bit I care about most.

Future writing should sound compatible with this. It should not copy the exact topic or sentence shapes, but it should preserve the same level of plainness, personal ownership, and conversational polish.

## Style Preferences

| Dimension | Preference |
|---|---|
| Perspective | Prefer first person when discussing Ben's own process, judgement, or experience. |
| Word choice | Prefer plain, practical words over elevated or literary phrasing. |
| Sentence flow | Prefer flowing sentences over clipped, choppy ones. |
| Confidence | Prefer clear, grounded confidence over heavy hedging, but avoid bombast. |
| Formality | Prefer natural contractions and written conversation over stiff formal prose. |
| Ownership | Prefer "me", "my", and "for me" when the point is personal. |
| Metaphor | Use simple, earned metaphors sparingly. Avoid decorative metaphors. |
| Endings | Prefer endings that leave room for judgement rather than sounding too final. |
| British texture | Phrases like "the bit" can be right when subtle. Avoid forcing slang. |

## Use Plain, Practical Language

Prefer words like:

- tidy up
- work out
- get down
- point out
- make clearer
- weak spots
- the bit
- probably
- usually
- I think

Avoid words like:

- articulate, when "say" works
- refine, when "tidy up" or "clean up" works
- utilise
- facilitate
- cohesive and impactful

## First Person

When an article is about Ben's experience, tools, workflow, parenting, health, home automation, technology, writing, or decision-making, default to first person.

Good:

> I think AI is most useful when it helps me clarify the argument without making the writing feel artificial.

Less good:

> AI is most useful when it helps the writer clarify the argument without making the writing feel artificial.

Use detached third person only when explaining general background, definitions, or external research.

## Flowing Sentences

Prefer prose that connects related thoughts into a smooth argument. Avoid making every sentence short for emphasis.

Good:

> Once my thoughts are on the page, the tool can help me tidy up the structure and make the argument easier to follow, which means I can focus on the bit I actually care about.

Less good:

> I put my thoughts on the page. The tool tidies the structure. The argument becomes easier to follow. I focus on the bit I care about.

## Confidence

Use hedges where they reflect real uncertainty or personal judgement. Do not use them as a nervous habit.

Good:

> The real work happens during the editing phase.

Also good:

> I think the real work usually happens during the editing phase.

Too weak:

> It seems like the real work probably might happen during the editing phase.

Too grand:

> The editing phase is where all meaningful writing is forged into its final form.

## Reflective Article Voice

Good:

> I think the danger with AI writing is that it can make the article feel finished before the thinking is finished. The words might look tidy, but the argument can still be vague underneath, and that is usually the part that matters most.

This works because it is plain, but still has a thoughtful centre. It does not just list steps.

## Avoid

- Corporate brochure language.
- Generic openings like "In today's fast-paced world".
- Over-polished three-part symmetry.
- Abstractions like authenticity, creativity, impact, and transformation without concrete meaning.
- Forced casualness or performed dialogue.
- Conclusions that sound too final or too resolved.

Bad:

> This approach empowers creators to leverage AI capabilities while preserving authentic human insight and maximising communicative impact.

Better:

> This works better for me because AI can help tidy up the structure without taking over the thinking.

## Article Generation Workflow

Before drafting, identify:

- What is Ben actually trying to say?
- What is the practical or personal reason this matters?
- What is the tension, uncertainty, or tradeoff?
- What would be too glib or too polished?
- What should remain unresolved or open to judgement?

Do not start with generic context. Start from the actual problem or observation.

After drafting, revise with these checks:

- Replace elevated words with plain ones.
- Add first-person ownership where the point is personal.
- Remove generic openings and conclusions.
- Break any sentence that is only long because it is trying to sound impressive.
- Keep flowing sentences where they reflect connected thinking.
- Add light hedging where the sentence sounds too absolute.
- Remove hedging where it weakens a point Ben actually believes.
- Replace abstract claims with concrete practical consequences.
- Remove any line that sounds like it could appear on a SaaS landing page.

Final voice check:

- Does this sound like clear written conversation?
- Does it preserve Ben's judgement?
- Does it use plain words?
- Does it avoid corporate polish?
- Does it feel reflective without becoming vague?
- Does it leave enough room for uncertainty where appropriate?
- Could Ben plausibly have written this after tidying up his own rough draft?
